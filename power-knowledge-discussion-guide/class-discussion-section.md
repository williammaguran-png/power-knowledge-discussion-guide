# Whole-Class Discussion Section

## 1. Question: "Why do you think male bodies became the 'default' or 'standard' in medical research?"

Possible student answers:
- "Because more men were in medical research and medical leadership."
- "People assumed male bodies were the norm, so they didn't think to study women separately."
- "Women's health was not prioritized because women had less power and influence in institutions."
- "It was cheaper and easier to study a more homogeneous group."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "Exactly. This shows how power shapes knowledge. The people designing studies, funding them, and setting medical standards were largely men, so their assumptions became the baseline. In other words, the 'normal' body was defined by the group with power. That's not objective science—it's biased science built on who had authority to define what counted as normal."

Example to add:
- "For years, heart disease was treated as a male condition, and the symptoms considered 'classic' were based on male experiences. Women were often diagnosed incorrectly because their symptoms were different. This shows how power and authority can make a narrow version of reality seem universal."

---

## 2. Question: "If the research was biased, why didn't doctors and patients notice sooner?"

Possible student answers:
- "Because the bias was built into medical training and textbooks."
- "People in power didn't want to question the system."
- "Women were dismissed, so their experiences were not trusted."
- "It became accepted as normal, so nobody challenged it."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "This is important because it shows how power makes bias invisible. The system didn't suddenly decide to be unfair—it gradually normalized a male-centered model of medicine. Doctors were taught that the male body was the standard, so they interpreted women's symptoms through that lens. If a woman said she had chest pain, nausea, or jaw pain, doctors often thought it was anxiety or indigestion, not a heart attack. The issue wasn't simply ignorance; it was a system of power that decided whose experiences counted as legitimate."

Example to add:
- "The term 'Yentl syndrome' was used by Dr. Bernadine Healy to describe how women had to present like men to receive the appropriate diagnosis. That phrase captures the problem perfectly: women had to fit the male model to be taken seriously."

---

## 3. Question: "Does this still happen today? Are there other areas where bias affects knowledge?"

Possible student answers:
- "Yes, in other health areas and in technology."
- "It still happens when certain groups are excluded from trials or data sets."
- "Bias affects education, AI, and public policy too."
- "The problem is not always obvious because it is hidden in systems."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "Yes. This pattern repeats across many areas. If the people with power define the standard, everyone else is measured against it. In medicine, it was men. In technology, it can be certain skin tones or accents. In education, it may be middle-class or Western forms of knowledge. The idea is the same: power decides what counts as normal, credible, or objective."

Example to add:
- "AI facial recognition systems historically worked less accurately on women and darker-skinned people because the training data was dominated by white male faces. The 'intelligence' of the system was based on a narrow sample. This demonstrates how data and knowledge are shaped by whose experiences are included."

---

## 4. Question: "If power shapes knowledge, does that mean there is no such thing as objective truth?"

Possible student answers:
- "There can still be truth, but our understanding is affected by bias."
- "Truth exists, but powerful groups shape what truths are prioritized."
- "We can aim for objectivity, but we must be aware of our own assumptions."
- "No, we should still be critical and question evidence."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "A good way to think about it is this: truth does not disappear, but access to truth is often distorted by power. The fact that women were misdiagnosed for years wasn't because heart disease wasn't real—it was because the research and medical systems were built around a limited model of the human body. So objectivity is not about pretending bias doesn't exist. It is about being transparent about bias, testing assumptions, and asking whose perspectives are missing."

Example to add:
- "The fact that women can experience heart attacks differently was real all along. It just wasn't recognized because the system was built on male-centered assumptions. Once research included women, the fuller truth emerged."

---

## 5. Question: "Do people with power have a responsibility to make sure knowledge is used responsibly?"

Possible student answers:
- "Yes, because they can have a bigger impact on society."
- "They should be more careful because their decisions affect others."
- "Not always, but if they are in charge they should be accountable."
- "Sometimes power is used to protect interests instead of truth."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "Yes. When people have power, their decisions often shape public life, health, and policy. That means they have a responsibility not only to produce knowledge but to use it ethically. If institutions ignore the risks or harms of their knowledge, then they become part of the problem. Power doesn't just allow people to shape knowledge—it also creates a duty to be accountable for the consequences of that knowledge."

Example to add:
- "Pharmaceutical companies and governments have been criticised for promoting or using medical research in ways that benefit their own interests rather than public health. When knowledge is controlled by powerful actors, it can be exploited, selectively shared, or ignored. That is why ethical responsibility matters."

---

## 6. Question: "Does power lead to 'ownership' of knowledge?"

Possible student answers:
- "Yes, because those with power control which knowledge gets recognized."
- "Knowledge can be owned by institutions, not just individuals."
- "Powerful groups decide what is seen as legitimate and what is ignored."
- "This is especially relevant to universities, governments, and corporations."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "Absolutely. Knowledge does not just belong to the person who discovers it. It is often controlled by institutions, governments, publishers, and corporations that decide which ideas are valued, funded, and circulated. This can create a kind of ownership over knowledge, where certain perspectives become dominant while others are excluded. In that way, power can decide not only what is known, but who gets to claim knowledge and whose knowledge is treated as authoritative."

Example to add:
- "Indigenous knowledge has often been dismissed or exploited by colonising powers, even when it was highly effective in areas such as agriculture, ecology, and medicine. The knowledge existed, but power determined whether it was recognized and respected."

---

## 7. Question: "What should people do when knowledge is produced by people with power or interests behind it?"

Possible student answers:
- "We need to ask critical questions about funding, purpose, and biases."
- "We should listen to multiple perspectives."
- "We need more transparency and accountability."
- "We should challenge dominant narratives instead of accepting them as obvious truth."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "Exactly. Critical thinking means not simply accepting knowledge because it comes from an authority figure or institution. We need to ask: who benefits from this knowledge? Who is missing from the research? What assumptions are hidden behind it? These questions do not make us cynical; they make us more responsible as consumers and producers of knowledge."

Example to add:
- "A study can be scientifically valid but still reflect biased assumptions if it excludes certain groups or frames the world from a narrow perspective. The right response is not to reject all knowledge, but to examine it critically and add missing perspectives."

---

## Concluding class question:

### "If power shapes knowledge, how can we become better thinkers and better citizens?"

Possible student answers:
- "By asking more questions and being aware of our biases."
- "By listening to more voices, especially those historically excluded."
- "By understanding that no one is completely objective, but we can aim for honesty and transparency."
- "By being critical without becoming cynical."

Suggested follow-up response:
- "That is the key takeaway. Power affects knowledge, but understanding that fact does not mean we should stop seeking truth. Instead, it means we should seek truth more carefully. We should be aware of bias, include multiple perspectives, and hold institutions accountable. In other words, we become better citizens when we recognize that knowledge is not neutral—it is shaped by power—and we take that seriously."

- "Knowledge is not only about discovering truth—it is also about who has the power to decide what counts as truth."
